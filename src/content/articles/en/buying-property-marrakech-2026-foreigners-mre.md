  <p>Two recent reforms directly affect how a foreigner or an MRE (Moroccan living abroad) buys property in Marrakech: law 41.25, which makes the <strong>authentic deed</strong> mandatory for any preliminary contract, and the new <strong>national real estate power-of-attorney registry</strong>, operational since 1 June 2026. A buyer using a power of attorney to purchase remotely must now register it before signing — or risk the file being blocked at the land registry.</p>
</div>

Signing a preliminary contract at a real estate agent's office, paying a deposit, and waiting for the file to move forward: this shortcut, still common a few years ago, now exposes a buyer in Marrakech to a simple risk — outright nullity of the agreement. Since law 41.25 took effect on 16 July 2026, only a deed received by a notary or an adoul protects the buyer. And since 1 June 2026, any power of attorney used to buy remotely must first be registered with a national registry before it produces any legal effect. Here is the full process, step by step, with realistic timelines and the documents to gather as a foreigner or a Moroccan living abroad (MRE).

## The steps of the buying process in Marrakech

**Step 1. Search, offer and preliminary checks.** Once the property is identified, the buyer makes an offer (generally not binding), then has the land title checked — personally or through a notary — at the ANCFCC, confirms there is no mortgage or opposition, and, for new-build, verifies the developer's legal existence.

**Step 2. Preliminary sale agreement before a notary or adoul.** Since law 41.25, this document must be received by a public officer. A preliminary contract signed only at an agency now has no legal value. It sets the price, the suspensive conditions (obtaining a mortgage, clearing pre-emption rights) and, often, an advance of 5 to 10% of the price for new-build properties.

**Step 3. Registering the power of attorney, if the buyer is acting remotely.** A foreigner or MRE who cannot be present in Morocco must have a power of attorney drawn up in authentic form, then have it registered at the competent court of first instance (the one named in the deed, or Rabat's court by default) — a step to complete before signing the preliminary contract, not after.

**Step 4. Transferring the funds.** Payment must go through a Moroccan bank account, ideally in foreign currency converted into a convertible account, to keep proof that the funds entered the country — an essential document if the buyer ever wants to resell and repatriate the proceeds.

![Branch office of ANCFCC, the body that registers every property purchase in Morocco.](/images/articles/procedure-achat-immobilier-marrakech-etranger-mre-2026.webp)

**Step 5. Signing the authentic deed.** The notary (for a titled property) or the adoul (for an untitled property, still common in parts of the medina) verifies that every suspensive condition has been met before drawing up the final deed and having the parties sign it.

**Step 6. Filing and registration at the ANCFCC.** The deed is then filed at the land registry to record the transfer of ownership. For a property already registered, this is generally quick; for one still undergoing first-time registration, the process follows a longer calendar, including a two-month opposition period after publication.

**Step 7. Handover of the keys and switching utilities.** Once the deed is signed, the buyer collects the keys and has water, electricity and, where applicable, the housing tax transferred into their name.

## The documents to gather before signing

<div class="article-checklist"><ul><li>Copy of a valid national ID card or passport.</li><li>Proof of address and, for an MRE, a consular card or foreign residence card.</li><li>Bank details (RIB) for a Moroccan account, ideally in convertible dirhams.</li><li>Proof of the origin and transfer of funds (international wire orders, bank attestations).</li><li>Power of attorney in authentic form, plus its national registry certificate, for any remote purchase.</li><li>Copy of the land title or, failing that, the moulkiya deeds, obtained directly from the seller or checked at the ANCFCC.</li></ul></div>

## The new power-of-attorney registry: what can stall an MRE's file

Before 1 June 2026, a power of attorney signed abroad was enough, once legalized, for a relative or an agent to sign on behalf of an absent buyer. That is no longer the case. The power of attorney must now be filed and registered with the clerk's office of the competent court of first instance, or it cannot be enforced against third parties — and the land registrar will refuse to record a sale backed by an unregistered mandate. The reform primarily targets property fraud, which has historically affected Moroccans living abroad and heirs to unsettled estates.

> "The mandate produces its legal effects only from the date of its registration."

In practice, for an MRE buying in Marrakech without being able to travel: the power of attorney must be drawn up in authentic form (before a notary, an adoul, or a Moroccan consulate abroad), then registered before the preliminary contract is signed — not just before the final deed. A file built in the reverse order can get stuck along the way.

## Realistic timelines, from preliminary contract to keys

A preliminary contract is legally valid from 30 days, but in practice 45 to 60 days is recommended to give the bank time to process a mortgage application and the notary time to check the file with the tax authorities and the land registry. A remote buyer must also plan ahead for registering the power of attorney, before even making an offer. For a property already registered in the seller's clear name, the whole process — from offer to keys — usually takes two to four months; a first-time land registration or an unsettled inheritance can add several more months.

The buying process in Marrakech is straightforward once these steps are known, but it tightened in 2026: two reforms that protect the buyer also demand more documentary rigor, especially for anyone buying from abroad. A file prepared in the right order — checks done, power of attorney registered, funds traceable — avoids most of the hold-ups seen in recent months.
