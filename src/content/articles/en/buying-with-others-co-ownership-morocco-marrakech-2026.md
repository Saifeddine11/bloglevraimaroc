Unmarried couples, brothers, friends or Moroccans living abroad pooling their funds: buying together is common in Marrakech, and it usually means buying **in co-ownership (indivision)**. It is easy to enter and hard to leave. This guide relies on the official text of the Real Rights Code for the basic rules and on expert commentary for the rest, and flags what we could not verify.

## What the law says: the right to leave at any time

Co-ownership is governed by Law No. 39-08 (Real Rights Code). The text published by the land registry (ANCFCC) sets three useful rules. **Article 25** lets co-owners divide the property if they unanimously agree. **Article 26** says that without agreement, the co-owner who wants out may apply to the court. **Article 27** sets the principle:

> "No one may be compelled to remain in co-ownership, and any co-owner may request partition." (our translation)

The same article lets co-owners agree in writing to stay together for a fixed term, which the court may shorten for a legitimate reason. The text we read contains no general majority rule for administering jointly owned property, so do not assume a majority is enough to decide.

## Set the shares according to real contributions

Each co-owner holds a share of the whole property, not a specific room. According to ReaConsult, if the deed says nothing, shares are presumed equal. A default 50/50 can then erase a real difference in contributions: the site gives the example of three buyers who funded 50%, 30% and 20% but were each registered for one third.

ReaConsult's advice, consistent across its two guides, is practical: align shares with contributions (down payment, share of the loan, costs), have the deed drawn up by a notary or an adoul, keep transfers and statements, and obtain a dated independent reference valuation at entry. This firm sells valuations, so treat this as good practice, not a legal requirement. The formalities of the deed are covered in our guide to [Law 41.25 and the authentic deed](/articles/loi-41-25-acte-authentique-immobilier-maroc). To compare with a company structure, see [buying in your own name or through a company](/articles/acheter-immobilier-maroc-nom-propre-ou-societe).

## Selling your share: pre-emption among co-owners

A co-owner can in principle sell their share, but not without consequences. The Real Rights Code (Article 292) defines **chefaa**, the right to acquire the share sold, and Article 293 requires being a co-owner at the time of the sale. A co-owner can therefore step into the shoes of your buyer.

The details are less certain. La Vie éco, citing Articles 296 and 304, says the pre-emptor must take the whole share sold, with one month after notification of the deed, one year otherwise, and four years if they never knew of the sale. Those article numbers do not appear in the extracts of the official text we could read: confirm them with a notary. LesMRE mentions a 30-day period, which differs. In any case, **always notify the other co-owners of your intention to sell**.

## The real risk: deadlock

According to lawyer Nabil Haddaji, quoted by Finances News Hebdo (2 June 2022), a co-owner, even a minority one, can disrupt a building permit, a fence or a bank loan, and a co-owned property sells below its real value. ReaConsult notes that selling the whole property needs everyone's consent: one refusal blocks it.

If disagreement persists, the courts are the route. According to ReaConsult, the judge appoints an expert, then orders partition in kind if the property is divisible, or a public auction (licitation) otherwise. A single villa or riad is rarely divisible. Finances News Hebdo gives an average of **two to three years** from filing to enforcement of the judgment, and LesMRE says one to three years for an auction. The capital stays locked meanwhile.

## Leaving amicably: buy-out and reference value

An amicable exit means a buy-out of shares, a sale to a third party with the price split, or a partition with a balancing payment (soulte). ReaConsult gives the formula: property value x share of the departing co-owner. The agreement is drawn up by a notary or adoul and then entered on the land title. On tax, two sites quote registration rates we could not cross-check: ask the notary for a costed estimate before committing. For notary fees, see [the notary's role and fees](/articles/notaire-achat-immobilier-maroc-role-honoraires).

## Checklist before buying together

- Write down who contributes what, and **align the shares** with those contributions.
- Have the deed drawn up by a **notary or adoul**, never by a simple private paper.
- Provide in writing the rules for buy-out, balancing payment and sale, **before** signing.
- Obtain a dated **reference valuation**, and keep every proof of payment.
- Decide who lives in the property, who pays the charges and who manages any rental.
- Check with the bank how a loan is granted to several borrowers.
- Consider a company if there are many parties or very unequal contributions.
- Check the property's status: see [inheriting property in Morocco](/articles/heritage-bien-immobilier-maroc-etranger-mre) if one buyer is an heir.

*This guide is informative and does not replace the advice of a notary, adoul or lawyer. Articles 296 and 304 and the partition duty rates were not verified in the official text.*
