A foreign buyer signing in Marrakech usually knows the price, the registration duties and the notary's fees. What comes as a surprise, often much later, is that two separate local taxes fall due every year — the housing tax and the municipal services tax — on a property they may occupy only a few weeks a year. And that the 75% allowance everyone mentions almost never applies to their situation. Here is the exact calculation, as set out in law no. 47-06.

## Two separate taxes, one shared base

The housing tax (taxe d'habitation, TH) is assessed in the name of the owner or usufructuary, failing that in the name of the holder or occupant (article 20 of law 47-06). The municipal services tax (taxe de services communaux, TSC) follows the same logic and covers built properties and constructions of every kind (article 33). Both apply within the perimeter of urban communes, in the peripheral zones defined by urban planning law 12-90, in designated centres, and in summer, winter and spa resorts.

The shared base is the **rental value**, set by comparison by the census commission, based on the average rents charged for similar dwellings in the same neighbourhood (article 23). It is therefore neither the purchase price nor the rent actually collected: it is an administrative value, revised every five years by a 2% increase.

One practical consequence is routinely overlooked: a property located in a summer, winter or spa resort is taxed **even when unoccupied** (article 25), and vacancy can then only be recognised under the strict conditions of articles 26-II and 31.

## The 2026 scale, applied bracket by bracket

Article 27 sets a progressive scale on the annual rental value:

| Annual rental value | TH rate |
|---|---|
| 0 to 5,000 MAD | exempt |
| 5,001 to 20,000 MAD | 10% |
| 20,001 to 40,000 MAD | 20% |
| Above 40,000 MAD | 30% |

The TSC is added at a flat rate (article 36): **10.50%** of the rental value for properties within the perimeter of urban communes, designated centres and summer, winter or spa resorts, and **6.50%** in the peripheral zones of urban communes. That distinction matters in Marrakech, where several recently developed areas sit outside the strict urban perimeter: how the plot is classified changes the TSC line by nearly 40%.

> For a property with an annual rental value assessed at 60,000 MAD, with no allowance: TH reaches 11,500 MAD (1,500 + 4,000 + 6,000 across the brackets) and TSC 6,300 MAD. Total: 17,800 MAD a year, before any co-ownership charges, insurance or maintenance.

Two safeguards exist: housing tax below 100 dirhams is not issued (article 28), and a rental value of 5,000 MAD or less cancels the TH — but not the TSC, which is due from the first dirham.

## The 75% allowance: the line separating an MRE from an investor

This is the most misunderstood point. Article 24 grants a 75% allowance on the rental value of the taxpayer's **primary residence** as owner or usufructuary. It extends, still on a primary-residence basis, to a dwelling occupied by the spouse, by ascendants or descendants in the direct line to the first degree, by co-owners in undivided ownership for the unit they occupy, and by members of certain property companies.

The law explicitly covers one scenario: **Moroccans residing abroad, for the dwelling they keep as their primary residence in Morocco, occupied free of charge by their spouse, ascendants or descendants in the direct line to the first degree.** An MRE who leaves their apartment to their parents therefore keeps the allowance. An MRE who rents it out, leaves it empty, or only visits in summer does not meet the condition.

For a foreign investor with no residential link to the property, or for a second home, **no allowance applies**: the TH is calculated on 100% of the rental value. The gap is substantial — on a rental value of 60,000 MAD, the allowance would bring the base down to 15,000 MAD and the TH to 1,000 MAD, against 11,500 MAD without it.

Worth noting: the allowance cannot be combined with any other reduction of this tax. It is also drafted for the housing tax, whereas article 35 bases the TSC on "the rental value serving as the basis for calculating" the taxes concerned — wording whose practical effect on the TSC line is worth checking directly on the tax notice rather than inferring.

## Where the bill drops, or disappears

Two mechanisms genuinely reduce the bill, each on conditions:

- **Five-year temporary exemption**: new constructions built by individuals as their primary residence are exempt from TH for the five years following the year of completion (article 22-II). The primary-residence condition again rules out a purely buy-to-let purchase — worth verifying before assuming that a [new-build apartment](/en/articles/new-apartment-marrakech-2026) is exempt by default.
- **Vacancy relief**: it requires a declaration filed during January of the year following the vacancy, with the local tax office and supported by evidence (inventory of fixtures and full removal of furniture in the case of repairs, removal of water and electricity meters for a unit awaiting allocation). Without that declaration, vacancy has no effect.

Two filing obligations underpin the rest: the declaration of completion of construction and the declaration of change of ownership or use, both due **by 31 January of the year following** the works or the change (article 30). A buyer who neglects the second remains exposed to a tax notice issued in the seller's name.

## Since June 2025, the counterparty is no longer the commune

Law no. 14-25, passed by the House of Representatives on 13 May 2025, amends and supplements law 47-06. It transfers collection of the housing tax and the municipal services tax from the Kingdom's General Treasury to the **General Tax Directorate (DGI)**, with a maximum of two months after publication in the Official Bulletin to transfer files and databases. The Director General of Taxes now replaces the Treasurer General before the courts in disputes concerning these two taxes.

Neither the rates nor the base change: only administration is centralised. For a non-resident owner, that means a claim against a rental value considered excessive, a request for relief and the payment itself now go through the DGI channel — which simplifies the remote management described in our guide to [co-ownership fees and property management in Marrakech](/en/articles/co-ownership-fees-property-management-marrakech).

## Checklist for a non-resident owner

- Ask for the rental value used by the administration **before** projecting a net yield: that figure, not the purchase price, drives both TH and TSC
- Check whether the plot falls within the urban perimeter (TSC at 10.50%) or a peripheral zone (6.50%)
- Do not count on the 75% allowance unless the property is genuinely a primary residence, including in the MRE case with free occupation by a first-degree relative
- File the change-of-ownership declaration by 31 January following the purchase
- In the event of vacancy, file the declaration in January of the following year, with evidence — relief cannot be obtained retroactively without it
- Build TH + TSC into the yield calculation alongside [rental income taxation](/en/articles/rental-withholding-tax-morocco-2026) and the [real estate profit tax](/en/articles/real-estate-profit-tax-morocco-calculation-exemptions) on resale

These two taxes do not undermine the case for investing in Marrakech, but they weigh on the net yield of a lightly occupied property — precisely the most common profile among foreign buyers. Building them into the purchase model, alongside the items detailed in our [guide to real estate taxation in Marrakech](/en/articles/real-estate-taxes-marrakech-morocco-2026), avoids an unpleasant surprise that returns every year.
