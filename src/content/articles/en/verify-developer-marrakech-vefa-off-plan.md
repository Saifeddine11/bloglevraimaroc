In Marrakech, a good share of new-build sales still happen off-plan, before the first wall goes up. The buyer signs, pays a deposit — sometimes tens of thousands of dirhams — on the strength of a floor plan and a sales brochure alone. In the vast majority of cases, the site progresses and the keys get handed over. But the Bab Darna scandal, which left roughly 800 reservation holders with no home and no refund, was a reminder that nothing required a developer to guarantee the funds collected before the law changed. Here is what to check before signing, what the law actually requires of a developer, and the signs that should make a buyer walk away.

## The case that changed the law: Bab Darna and its 800 reservation holders

The Bab Darna case remains the reference point. Its developer, Mohammed El Ouardi, was arrested along with several associates for fraud after collecting advance payments from hundreds of families without ever guaranteeing completion of the works or the return of the funds. Nearly 800 victims were still awaiting a ruling, each having paid roughly half the value of their property before being left with neither a home nor a refund.

> "The people responsible exploited legal gaps by collecting buyers' advance payments with no guarantee of completion and no guarantee of reimbursement."

The scandal pushed the government to work on a decree requiring a mandatory insurance-backed guarantee for the reimbursement of advance payments. This context, more than the law itself, explains why verifying the developer must come before any signature: the law offers better protection than before 2016, but not against a developer who sells land he doesn't own, or who simply lacks the means to finish the site.

## Verifying the developer's legal existence and track record

Before discussing guarantees or payment schedules, the first check concerns the very existence of the company the buyer is about to contract with — not just the trade name displayed on the site hoarding.

- **The ICE number and trade register entry** of the selling company (often a dedicated project company, distinct from the historic developer group). An online ICE checker can confirm that a company is genuinely registered under that exact name.
- **The land title of the plot**: registered and clear, in the name of the selling company, with no mortgage or ongoing dispute. This is exactly what was missing in a case in El Jadida, where a developer sold plots the bank had already transferred to another operator after a seizure — over 10 million dirhams committed by buyers before he fled.
- **The building permit**, displayed on the site board and checkable with the local municipality or urban planning agency: without it, no construction is legal, whatever progress is visible.
- **The developer's track record**: projects already delivered, deadlines actually met, feedback from past buyers. A recent developer with no delivered project isn't disqualifying on its own, but it calls for extra vigilance on the points below.

## The guarantees the law actually requires from the developer

Off-plan sales (VEFA) in Morocco are governed by law no. 44-00 of 3 October 2002, amended by law no. 107-12 of 2016. This text sets a capped payment schedule — not the "20/30/30/20" still quoted by many sales guides:

| Stage | Legal cap |
| --- | --- |
| Reservation contract | 5% |
| Preliminary contract (or 10% without a prior reservation) | 5% to 10% |
| Start of works | 10% |
| Three progress phases (foundations, structural work, finishing/habitation permit) | 60% total |
| Final deed of sale and handover of keys | 20% |

On top of this, four legal guarantees must be explicitly provided for in the contract: a one-year perfect completion guarantee covering defects noted at handover, a two-year guarantee on detachable fittings (shutters, heating, air conditioning), a ten-year guarantee on the structural soundness of the building, and a bank guarantee or mortgage covering the return of funds paid if the developer abandons the site. This last guarantee is precisely the one that was missing in the Bab Darna affair — it's what the draft decree mentioned above aims to fix. A buyer should ask, before signing, to see this document and the name of the guaranteeing bank or insurer, not just a generic mention in the contract.

## Warning signs to never ignore

Some behaviors, taken alone, prove nothing; taken together, they should stop a signature in its tracks.

- **A private agreement with no notary or adoul.** The VEFA preliminary contract must be drawn up by a licensed professional; a simple "private" agreement between individuals doesn't carry the same legal weight and strips the buyer of the protection of the official text.
- **A request to pay in cash or into a personal account** rather than the project company's account identified on the land title — exactly the mechanism that let the El Jadida developer disappear with the funds.
- **A price or discount that's "too good"** on a project with no track record, especially paired with pressure to sign quickly before an announced price rise.
- **No bank guarantee document at all** despite an explicit request, or evasive answers about the identity of the guaranteeing bank.
- **A land title not yet registered in the developer's name**, or a plot still encumbered by an unlifted mortgage.

## Checklist before signing a reservation contract

- Verify the ICE number and trade register entry of the exact selling company (not just the group's trade name).
- Request a copy of the land title for the plot and confirm it is clear, in the seller's name, with no ongoing mortgage.
- Require the number and date of the building permit, checkable with the local municipality or urban planning agency.
- Have the contract drawn up by a notary or an adoul — never a simple private agreement.
- Ask for the bank guarantee or mortgage document covering the reimbursement of funds paid, and the name of the guaranteeing institution.
- Check that the proposed payment schedule respects the legal cap (5% / 5-10% / 10% / 60% / 20%) and never asks for a disproportionate upfront payment.
- Never pay in cash or into any account other than the one belonging to the company identified on the land title.
- Look into projects already delivered by the same developer before committing to a project with no track record.

None of these checks alone guarantees a project will be completed on the announced timeline — even a solid developer can run late. But together they eliminate the great majority of risks that have led, in the past, to scandals like Bab Darna: the absence of a financial guarantee, and the absence of prior verification of the company the buyer is actually contracting with.
