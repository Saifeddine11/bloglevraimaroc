Two neighbouring plots at the same price per square metre can have nothing in common: one can carry several storeys, the other is classed as agricultural or burdened by a planned road. Nothing shows on site. In Marrakech, it is zoning, not the view, that sets a plot's value.

## Why "buildable" land is not always buildable

The main danger fits in one sentence: paying a buildable-land price for a plot that cannot legally be built on. According to ReaConsult, once the deed is signed the gap between the price paid and the real value is "almost never recoverable". Urban planning is governed by law 12-90, which sets what each zone allows.

The warning signs listed by the same source are simple:

- the seller cannot produce a recent urban information note for the exact plot;
- the land is sold as "soon to be buildable" or "future reclassification";
- the price is unusually low for the area;
- you are pressed to sign quickly;
- the surrounding area is sparsely built.

> A check costs a few thousand dirhams; the mistake can cost a large share of the land price.

## The NRU: the document to demand before negotiating

![Reviewing and signing a legal document linked to a land purchase](/images/articles/procedure-achat-immobilier-maroc-etape-par-etape-2.webp)

The note de renseignements urbanistiques (NRU, urban information note) is issued by the municipality's planning department. It translates the development plan's rules for one specific plot. According to ReaConsult, it states in particular:

- the **zoning** (residential, mixed, commercial, industrial, agricultural);
- the **COS** (floor area allowed per m² of land) and the **CES** (maximum ground footprint);
- the maximum **height**, in storeys and metres;
- the **setbacks** from roads and neighbours;
- **reserved easements** for future public works, and alignment constraints.

It reflects the rules on the date it is issued: plans can be revised, so an old note may be outdated. Classic mistakes are mixing up an approved zone with a "proposed" one, simply multiplying COS by surface without deducting setbacks and easements, or assuming the NRU lists every servitude (some appear only on the title). Cross-check against the surface on the land title, which can differ from the measured one.

For doubtful cases, ReaConsult says a buildability appraisal starts at 3,500 MAD excl. VAT, with a standard turnaround of 5 to 8 days (48 to 72 hours if urgent).

## Marrakech: recent plans, a master plan still pending

![Marrakech infrastructure, a city whose expansion is framed by new development plans](/images/articles/marrakech-2030-infrastructures-tourisme.webp)

Marrakech's zoning has moved a lot. Médias24 lists development plans adopted for Tassoultant (2017), Mechouar El Kasba and Sidi Youssef Ben Ali (2021), the former industrial district (2023) and Guéliz-Est (2024).

The latest, for **Marrakech-West and M'hamid Sud**, was approved by the municipal council on 23 June 2025 and then homologated by decree in Official Bulletin no. 7496. According to Médias24 it covers 40% of the city's area (the Ménara district and western Guéliz), plans 466 green spaces totalling about 100 hectares, and declares the expropriations needed for its facilities to be of public utility, under article 28 of law 12-90. In other words, a plot in this sector may be hit by a future easement, hence the value of a recent NRU.

On the outskirts the picture is less clear. The master plan (SDAU) for Greater Marrakech is meant to replace the 1995 one across 2,332 km² (11 prefecture communes and 8 in Al Haouz province). Its tender has been relaunched for the third time in a year, with the study cut to 15 months. In Tassoultante, despite a 2017 plan, residents cannot obtain permits: about 43 douars have a legally ambiguous land status, neither collective nor private. For a buyer, a "cheap" outlying plot can therefore be administratively blocked. See also our [guide to land status](/articles/buy-land-marrakech-legal-status-foreign-buyers).

## From plot to permit: timelines, costs, Rokhas

![Marrakech railway station, a symbol of the big projects reshaping the city](/images/articles/marrakech-perspectives-marche-2026-2030.webp)

Building permits fall under law 12-90, supplemented by law 55-19 on simplifying procedures. According to DémarchesMaroc:

- a maximum legal delay of **30 days** after a complete file is filed, about 15 days in practice through the Rokhas.ma platform;
- validity of **one year**, with works to start within that period;
- an architect is mandatory above **240 m²** of covered area;
- a per-m² fee: 30 MAD/m² for mid-to-high-range villas and apartments.

Rokhas.ma became the mandatory filing channel (2020 Interior Ministry circular, about 1,000 local authorities at the time). Bladi noted then that some permits exceeded 150 days in certain municipalities, against a norm of 30 days on average.

Latest change: dahir no. 1.26.61 of 28 July 2026 (law 34.21 amending law 25.90, Official Bulletin no. 7533 of 10 August 2026) allows some permits before all of a subdivision's utilities are finished. This exemption targets public operations only (rehousing after disasters, slum clearance, public-utility projects) and keeps roads, water, electricity and sanitation mandatory. It changes nothing for buying a private lot: always ask for proof that the utilities are in place.

## Checklist before signing

- Demand a recent NRU for the exact plot, issued by the municipality, and compare it with the approved development plan (not the draft).
- Compute buildable area after deducting setbacks and reserved easements, not by simple COS × surface.
- Check at the land registry that the title's surface and charges match reality.
- Ask whether the plot lies in a recent plan sector (Marrakech-West, Guéliz-Est, etc.) where future easements are possible.
- On the outskirts, confirm the land status before thinking about a permit (the Tassoultante case).
- Budget the permit fee (about 30 MAD/m² for a villa) and an architect if the covered area exceeds 240 m².
- If in doubt, commission a buildability appraisal before paying any deposit.

*This article is informational and does not replace advice from a notary, an architect or the urban agency. Timelines and rules vary by municipality; check with the authorities.*
