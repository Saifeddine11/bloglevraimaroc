In Marrakech, many owners only discover that a mandatory insurance guarantee exists at the moment of a claim — or a denied payout. Since law No. 110-14 came into force, a natural-catastrophe guarantee has been folded by right into several insurance contracts in Morocco, without the policyholder always realizing its real scope. Here is what the law actually requires from a property owner in Marrakech, what stays a matter of individual choice, and the specific case of condominiums.

## What law 110-14 has required since 2020

Law No. 110-14, in force since January 2020, created a regime in Morocco covering the consequences of catastrophic events — floods, earthquakes, and landslides among others. Its central mechanism, set out in article 64-1 of the Insurance Code, makes the "natural catastrophe" guarantee **mandatory within certain existing contracts**, rather than a standalone policy every owner would need to take out on their own initiative.

Concretely, this guarantee is automatically folded into insurance contracts that already cover a property against fire or water damage, as well as into contracts tied to a mortgage or a lease-purchase agreement. A property owner in Marrakech who took out a bank loan to finance their purchase is therefore, in practice, covered against natural catastrophes through the insurance their bank required. By contrast, an owner who paid cash, without home insurance or an associated loan, is not automatically covered: the law does not create a standalone obligation to insure — it attaches the guarantee to contracts that already exist.

> "The regime covering the consequences of catastrophic events rests on two pillars: an insurance-based regime for people already covered, and a solidarity fund against catastrophic events (FSEC) for people without coverage."

This second pillar, the FSEC, compensates uninsured people at a flat-rate level — a minimal safety net, far below compensation calculated on a property's real value.

## What stays at the owner's discretion

Outside the natural-catastrophe guarantee attached to existing contracts, Morocco has no general legal requirement to take out comprehensive home insurance for a property owned outright and free of any mortgage. A property owner in Marrakech who owns their home without an ongoing loan can legally remain without fire, water-damage, or liability cover — a frequent choice, but one that leaves the entire risk on their own shoulders in the event of a claim.

This absence of a general obligation explains part of what is seen in Marrakech: old riads in the medina, villas in the Palmeraie, or apartments bought in cash sometimes remain minimally insured, or not insured at all, while homes financed through a loan mechanically benefit from broader cover imposed by the lending bank.

## The specific case of condominiums

![Signing a legal document related to a property purchase in Morocco](/images/articles/assurance-habitation-marrakech-proprietaire-2026-2.webp)

For condominium buildings — the most common situation for an apartment in Marrakech — law No. 18-00 governs collective decision-making, including on insuring the building. Taking out a policy covering shared areas is not imposed by a dedicated article, but it falls among the decisions that require a qualified majority at the co-owners' general meeting: article 21 of law 18-00 sets the required majority at three-quarters of the votes for major decisions, a category that includes insuring the building alongside voting the budget or electing the property manager (syndic).

In practice, this means a single co-owner cannot impose — or block — taking out insurance for shared areas: the decision is made collectively, and a syndic cannot commit the condominium on this point without the general meeting's approval. For a non-resident investor buying in Marrakech without personally attending general meetings, this point deserves particular attention, since the absence of a building policy exposes all co-owners in the event of a claim affecting the structure or shared areas. ## The riad case: specific vigilance required

![Traditional riad courtyard in Marrakech with zellige tilework and columns](/images/articles/assurance-habitation-marrakech-proprietaire-2026-3.webp)

Medina riads raise a difficulty that modern apartments do not face: traditional materials (rammed earth, wood, carved plaster), a sometimes-old structure, and frequently no formal syndic since they are often individual properties rather than vertical condominiums. An owner who wants to insure a riad generally needs the property assessed case by case by the insurer, since a riad's rebuilding value with zellige and traditional plaster does not fit the standard grids designed for a concrete building.

This specificity does not lighten any legal obligation: a riad financed through a mortgage remains subject to the same article 64-1 as any other property, with the natural-catastrophe guarantee folded into the insurance contract the bank requires. For a renovation project, our [guide to renovating a riad in the Marrakech medina](/articles/renover-riad-medina-marrakech-2026) details the real budget and the permits needed before work starts — factors that directly affect the insurer's assessment.

## Why this matters particularly for non-resident owners

For a Moroccan living abroad (MRE) or a foreign investor who owns property in Marrakech without living there year-round, going without voluntary insurance is a concrete risk: water damage or an electrical incident in a property left unoccupied for months can worsen considerably before being detected, with no legal mechanism requiring cover if the property was bought without a loan. Checking the insurance status — of the property itself, and of the shared areas in a condominium — belongs among the checks to run alongside the land title or condominium charges before any purchase.

## What to remember before acting

- A mortgage or lease-purchase agreement automatically triggers the natural-catastrophe guarantee through the insurance the bank requires (article 64-1 of the Insurance Code)
- Without a loan, Morocco has no general legal requirement to take out home insurance for a property owned outright
- In a condominium, insuring shared areas is decided by a three-quarters majority at the general meeting (article 21, law 18-00) — a single co-owner can neither impose it nor block it alone
- For a riad, the rebuilding value needs a case-by-case assessment with the insurer, due to the traditional materials involved
- With no insurance and no loan, only the solidarity fund (FSEC) pays out, at a flat-rate level, for a recognized natural catastrophe

## What still needs case-by-case verification

The regime under law 110-14 covers events officially recognized as catastrophic by the state, following a formal declaration procedure — it is not automatic coverage for every weather-related claim. The precise triggering conditions, the FSEC's compensation caps, and the exact content of the folded-in guarantees vary by contract: they should be checked directly with the insurer or, for a condominium, in the building's bylaws and general-meeting minutes.
